Speed matters because water does not stay where it lands. Within hours it wicks into drywall, spreads under flooring and reaches the framing. Removing the bulk of it early is what keeps a one-room problem from becoming a whole-floor rebuild. This page covers the removal step. Drying the structure afterward is a separate job, described on the structural drying page.

When standing water is an emergency

Any water deeper than a puddle, any water that is still rising, and any water that reached an outlet, a furnace or a breaker panel should be treated as urgent. Water that has been standing for a day or more, or that came from a drain or sewer line, is a bigger concern because contamination climbs the longer it sits.

If the water touches electrical equipment, stay out, shut off power at the panel only if you can reach it while standing on a dry surface, and call the utility or 911 before you call us. Gas smell or a sparking outlet is never something to wait out.

Safe first steps while you wait

Close the main water valve if a pipe or fixture is feeding the water.

Photograph the room before anything is moved. Wide shots first, then close-ups of the waterline.

How water is removed from a Lakewood home

Crews start by finding the water's depth and spread, then choose equipment to match. Deep water goes out first with submersible pumps. Once the depth drops to a thin film, truck-mounted or portable extraction units and wet vacuums pull water from carpet, pad, tile grout and hard floors.

Moisture meters and thermal imaging then show where water traveled out of sight, such as under baseboards, inside a wall cavity or beneath a vinyl floor. That reading decides what is removed and what can be dried in place.

  • Submersible and trash pumps for depth over an inch or two.
  • Extraction wands and weighted tools for saturated carpet and pad.
  • Wet vacuums for tile, concrete and corners the larger units cannot reach.
  • Moisture meters and a thermal camera to confirm what is still wet after the visible water is gone.

What counts as extraction and what does not

Extraction removes liquid water. It does not dry the building. A floor can look clear and still hold water in the subfloor, the pad or the lower wall. Anyone who tells you the job is finished after a wet vac pass has skipped the part that prevents mold.

After extraction, the usual next steps are drying with air movers and dehumidifiers, removing materials that cannot be saved, and cleaning. If the water came from a plumbing failure, a plumber still has to repair the pipe. If it came from rain or a rising source, the flood damage page covers that situation.

Questions about water extraction in Lakewood

How quickly should standing water be removed?

As soon as it is safe. Water moves into drywall, flooring and framing within hours, and every extra day raises the chance of mold and the amount that must be torn out. If you cannot start pumping yourself, call the line and shut off whatever source you can reach.

Can I just use a shop vacuum?

A shop vacuum helps with a thin film on a hard floor, but it cannot move the volume in a flooded room and it does nothing for water inside carpet pad or wall cavities. If electrical outlets are wet, do not plug anything in.

Is extraction the same as drying?

No. Extraction removes liquid water. Drying uses air movers and dehumidifiers to remove moisture held in materials. Most wet buildings need both, in that order.

Will extraction save my carpet?

Sometimes. Clean water caught within a day can often be extracted from carpet, while the pad underneath is commonly replaced. Water from a drain or sewer is a different decision and usually means removal.
