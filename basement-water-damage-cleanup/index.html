A flooded basement is an emergency because the water sits next to your furnace, water heater and electrical panel, and because it soaks everything stored below. If water is coming in, call (216) 541-8541. The line is answered 24/7, and the first steps are to keep out of the water, turn off the power from a dry place and find out where it is coming from.

This page covers the below-grade room: floors, walls, stored contents and finished space. The crawl space under part of a house has its own page on crawl space water damage, and an outside flood event is on flood damage cleanup.

Water in the basement right now

If water is rising, stay out unless you can reach the panel dry. Never walk into a flooded basement with the power on. Call the utility or 911 if you suspect electrical contact or smell gas. Move to the main floor anything you can safely carry and keep people and pets away from the water.

Do not pump the basement out too fast

Removing water from a flooded basement too quickly while the ground outside is saturated can stress foundation walls. A crew pumps in stages, and you should ask about it before running your own pump.

How the cleanup works

Crews pump the water, remove wet contents and cut out wet drywall and insulation on finished walls. Concrete block and poured walls are cleaned and dried, and a moisture meter checks the floor, the walls and the bottom plates of any framed walls. Carpet is removed in most flood cases.

Dehumidifiers matter most here because basement air is damp and cool. A drying plan combines dehumidification, airflow and heat where needed, and the crew keeps readings until the space is at its goals.

Restoration is not waterproofing

Pumping and drying remove the water that came in. They do not stop more from coming in. If the cause is seepage, a failing sump or a cracked wall, a waterproofing or foundation contractor handles the fix. We say this plainly because a wet basement that is only dried will fill again after the next heavy rain.

Lakewood basements and the sewer system

Lakewood homes are old, with a median build year near 1938 in Census survey data, and many have full basements with a floor drain. The city says groundwater and stormwater that enter aging sewers use up capacity and can cause basement flooding when the sanitary system overloads. The city also says it owns the sewer mains while the owner is responsible for the private side. A plumber's camera shows which side holds the problem.

A floor drain that smells but is not backing up may simply be dry. The city recommends adding a small bucket of water to each floor drain about once a week, especially in winter.

Finished basements and stored contents

Finished basements hold drywall, trim, carpet and furniture, all of which wick water. Boxes of paper, photos and clothes are best sorted early, and photographing items before disposal helps with an insurance claim. Items touched by sewage are generally discarded, while hard goods can often be cleaned.

Questions about basement water damage in Lakewood

Is it safe to go into a flooded basement?

Not with the power on or with water over outlets. Keep out until the electrical service is off from a dry location or the utility has checked it.

Will drying stop my basement from flooding again?

No. Drying removes the water that came in. Preventing repeats depends on the source, such as a sump, drainage or a sewer line.

Can I use my own pump?

A small pump can help, but pumping too fast on saturated ground may stress walls. Ask a crew about staging the removal.

Who is responsible for the sewer line?

The city owns the mains and owners are responsible for the pipe on private property. A camera inspection shows where the issue is.

Do you handle basement water damage emergencies at night or on weekends in Lakewood?

Yes. Basements flood during overnight storms, so the line at (216) 541-8541 is answered 24/7. Same-day response is offered when crews are available, and staying out of the water until the power is off is the first rule.
